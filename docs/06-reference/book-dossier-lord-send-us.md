## Chronological timeline (merged, deduplicated, with years and book page refs)

- **1960** — Carole's parents leave for the Philippines as missionaries (photo caption, p. 185).
- **Carole's childhood (1960s-70s)** — Grows up in jungle villages of the Philippines among Muslim Abu Sayyaf and communist terrorists; her father is a Wycliffe Bible translator who never flees danger, praying and fasting instead (pp. 81-82, 175).
- **~1975 ("about 50 years ago")** — A lone missionary comes to the jungle mountains of eastern South Sudan, stays 17 years, and wins only one convert (p. 182).
- **Late 1980s-1990s (Sudan civil war)** — Kong, at ages 12-14, leads the "Lost Boys of Sudan" on foot through the mountains of Sudan and Ethiopia to freedom; one year too old to be sent abroad, he is forced into the South Sudan military and contracts hepatitis B in war (p. 169).
- **~1987-2009** — Joseph Kony's LRA wages a devastating 20-22 year war in northern Uganda; research counts 66,000 children abducted as child soldiers; the UN calls it the worst atrocity since Hitler; Kony abducts his own nephew Okelo at age 12 and trains him in human sacrifice under the demonic agent Akim (pp. 44, 130, 181).
- **2002** — An Australian Christian child psychologist writes his trauma healing program for former child soldiers; other organizations refuse it (p. 130).
- **~2002-2004 ("more than 20 years ago" / "22 years ago" at writing)** — Carole receives her call to Africa while washing dishes at her kitchen sink in Oklahoma; God gives her three "walking on water" lessons; she steps off the plane praying "Lord send me where no one wants to go" despite warnings she would come home in a body bag; the region has been racked by war for 30 years (pp. 9, 175-176). God's first two words to her in Africa: "strategize and mobilize" (p. 182). God gives her Zechariah 2:5 ("a wall of fire around her") as her protection verse (p. 44).
- **~2002 onward (LRA war years)** — Carole lives in Gulu, northern Uganda, missing ambushes by minutes while driving into the camps; she never uses security escorts, only Psalm 91 (p. 14). A bishop from Gana warns her (~20 years before writing) that transformational ministry will provoke severe occult opposition; she should get the strongest prayer covering in Uganda and America (pp. 143-144).
- **2004** — Carole is the only Western woman living alone in Gulu (the "crazy white lady"); about 16 traumatized people stay in her little house; the Australian doctor finds her and Favor implements the first trauma healing program, never stopped since (pp. 131-132). She takes in Innocent, an escaped child soldier from Kony's army, as her first child soldier (pp. 172-173). She spends weeks alone on the Nile River in prayer and writes a strategic vision during wartime; God fulfills almost everything in it 10 years later (p. 93).
- **~2004-2005 ("20 years ago")** — Carole begins taking in the first war orphans and abandoned children in Gulu, including a five-year-old boy found sitting on his mother's grave and four girls (ages ten, eight, six, and a newborn) surviving on garbage scraps (p. 171).
- **Early Gulu years (undated)** — Sadie, age 82, met at a Pennsylvania prayer retreat, writes Carole a $100,000 check, the first offering of its kind Carole had received; it pays for the orphanage property, first buildings, the beginning of the school, and widow/orphan ministries. Sadie dies during a minor knee operation before her planned trip to Africa (pp. 199-202).
- **~2005** — Moses, a Darfurian and former Janjaweed (Rapid Forces) rebel militia leader, gives his life to Jesus after God calls him "shepherd" in a dream; a South Sudanese chaplain leads him to Christ; he starts a Darfurian church even after his wife and children are abducted by Muslims and never found (pp. 115-116, 121).
- **~2006 ("20 years ago" at writing)** — The Gulu House of Prayer is packed with people on their knees travailing (p. 181).
- **2007** — First national prayer gathering held in Uganda; credited with stopping the war (p. 30). A second gathering is credited with stopping Ebola in five days (p. 30).
- **Undated (post-conversion era, before 2017)** — Okelo repeatedly attempts to assassinate Carole but is blocked by a wall of fire; converted at the House of Prayer after a word of knowledge exposes him; discipled 2 years, then 2 years of Bible school in southern Uganda, graduating top of his class (pp. 44-45). After returning to Gulu he confronts the city's four gang leaders (each commanding 250 youth) over three consecutive nights; ~50 gang members come to the House of Prayer the first day, over 100 by week's end, 100 baptized, 120 teenagers move onto a new property; a 10-acre unfinished hotel is bought ($500 down, two years interest-free) to house 500-1,000 girls (pp. 46-52).

After the first National Prayer Gathering, Carole meets Moses and preaches at his tarp-canopy slum church of Darfurian believers in Juba (30-40 chairs); she buys chairs and prays over church land (pp. 121-122).
- **~2015 ("ten years ago")** — Carole holds a crusade in Rumbek on a field cursed by human sacrifice, confronts the region's strongest witch doctor (an old one-eyed woman) who orders the team killed, and dedicates the first House of Prayer and mission station in South Sudan before 600 people (pp. 152-154).

Favor buys its first piece of land (p. 122) and opens the House of Prayer in Juba on purchased land (p. 145). First edition of the book published (pp. 4, 44).

## Carole's story arc (chapter-by-chapter factual narrative)

**Foreword and calling.** Carole Ward, raised in the Philippine jungle as the daughter of a Wycliffe Bible translator who never fled from terrorists, was called to Africa more than twenty years before writing while washing dishes at her kitchen sink in Oklahoma. She stepped off the plane praying "Lord send me where no one wants to go," despite warnings she would come home in a body bag, into a region racked by thirty years of war. She wanted to live in one village and pray down revival, but God answered: train 1,000 people and reach 1,000 villages instead of one. Ten years in, He added a second directive: "Multiply and accelerate for time is short." At the time of writing, close to 1,000 indigenous missionaries from almost 100 language groups work in over seven nations (foreword, pp. 9-11).

**Chapter 1: the road to Juba.** After the July 2016 fighting that killed thousands in Juba and drove inflation to nearly 180%, Carole drove from Gulu into South Sudan's capital, her first military escort in almost fifteen years (three armed trucks, about 25 soldiers), passing burned villages to deliver Bibles, rice, beans, and radio supplies to her Sudanese leadership team at the radio station and House of Prayer. Her pastors were stunned she came by road; a hardened commander wept over brother killing brother, and Carole promised peace would come through Jesus Christ. Her anchors: Isaiah 33:6, Colossians 3:3, and the conviction that no one can live for something they are not willing to die for (pp. 13-19).

**Chapter 2: "Come Dead or Alive."** After South Sudan's 2011 birth, the team was led to the Toposa tribe in Kapoeta, Eastern Equatoria, seven hours away by dangerous road, after three weeks of fasting. At a two-week Favour Gospel Crusade more than 800,000 people came, many walking up to seven days. A dead nine-year-old girl was raised to life, two blind men were healed, a man crippled from birth walked, and rain ended a nine-month drought after public prayer. The Governor publicly declared he wanted to serve this God, then asked the team to bring peace to a "killer tribe" four hours into the mountains; three missionaries and a soldier-translator went, shared a simplified Gospel with the chief, and the whole tribe responded (pp. 20-27).

**Chapter 3: prayer is not optional.** Told Juba land would cost at least $5 million, Carole found a central-Juba warehouse property and bought it for $80,000 with only $2,000 down, interest-free over two years, opening a House of Prayer after renovations. The chapter sets prayer as the ministry's engine: the 2007 Uganda national prayer gathering credited with stopping the war, another credited with stopping Ebola in five days, continuous South Sudan gatherings from 2015, and the 2019 77-hour national prayer guide that filled Juba's largest stadium, followed the next day by the removal of a 30-year dictator and the end of Christian persecution. Twice the Juba property was seized at gunpoint by soldiers through bribed judges; a 40-day fast (gates reopened day 42) and later a six-hour turnaround restored the title both times, with both judges arrested (pp. 28-35).

**Chapter 4: healing the heart of a nation.** To address the 60-plus-year Dinka-Nuer blood feud, made raw by the 2016 killings and the 2017 UN protection camps, Carole wrote five letters to the UN until Nuer camp residents were permitted to attend a week-long worship conference. About 400 Dinka and 400 Nuer met for five days in a 1,000-seat church; Empower trauma-healing skits using bricks and five-pound notes taught forgiveness, ending in weeping embraces. Days later a UN letter reported a radical change in the camps; a second letter two months on confirmed lasting peace with no more killings. "The Lord did in 2 hours what no one could have done in years" (pp. 36-42).

**Chapter 5: prostitutes to prayer warriors, prisoners to preachers.** Okelo, nephew of LRA leader Joseph Kony, abducted at twelve and trained in human sacrifice, was sent repeatedly to assassinate Carole but was stopped each time by a wall of fire (Zechariah 2:5). Converted at the House of Prayer, discipled two years, and a top Bible school graduate, he returned to Gulu, mapped the city's four gang lords (250 youth each), and over three 2 a.m. visits won them over. Fifty gang members came to lunch the first day, over 100 by week's end; 100 were baptized and 120 teenagers moved onto a newly opened property, followed by a 10-acre unfinished hotel purchased with $500 down for the girls. God told Carole He turns "murderers into missionaries, prostitutes into prayer warriors, prisoners into preachers" (pp. 44-54).

**Chapter 6: "Please Mama, save my legs!"** In Juba's market, ten-year-old Peter, with deep infected leg wounds, begged Carole to save his legs. She took him to Favor's 20x20-meter compound mid-construction, and the next morning, at Peter's plea, staff rescued 25 of his friends from under the bridges. Radio staffers Martin and Albert became the boys' first "fathers"; within two months the boys were leading worship and refused to be called orphans "because they had found their Daddy God." The team then found nearly 20 locations around Juba to feed and disciple street children (pp. 55-62). At a Bible school graduation, government vice president Rebecca Garan saw the transformed boys and begged Carole never to leave (p. 61).

**Chapters 7-8: the unreached tribes.** In 2019 a Tennessee pastor and two church members joined five Favor missionaries under Yumba on a trek through lion country and swamps to the hidden Kachipo tribe; a witch-doctor chief had dreamed of their coming and welcomed them; over 200 were saved, about 70 baptized, more villages evangelized, portable Bible schools and a bamboo church established, followed by a mobile medical clinic and a planned Village Learning Center. The same year God sent Carole to the Murle, called by media the most dangerous tribe in the world: her two government translators plotted to kill her and were diverted; her convoy passed unharmed down a road where a food convoy had been massacred two days earlier; a week-long crusade saw thousands saved and children covenanted to Satan delivered; the governor offered land for a school. Back in Juba, Emma, a Murle ex-prisoner who had graduated from the Portable Bible School inside Juba prison, was commissioned as missionary to his own people and went on to plant churches toward the Ethiopian border (pp. 63-81).

**Chapter 9: pregnant with vision.** God sent Terry, a Western Canadian, who mastered the administration of Gulu in months, freeing Carole to push north; by then Carole carried responsibility for over 1,000 people across 27 departments. At a Habakkuk 2:2 leadership retreat the indigenous leaders wrote down God's vision; Yumba announced the REEP (Rural Evangelism and Empowerment Program) expansion from the 10 states of South Sudan to 10 more nations in the 10/40 window. Carole wrestled with God's warning that a prayer movement become mission movement may become a martyrdom movement. During Covid, the team defied lockdown with a 40-day, eight-hour-a-day fast in a tent in Juba; the armed security sent to stop them became their protectors (pp. 88-97).

**Chapters 10-11: from terrorism to triumph.** On the Gulu-Juba road, two rebels disguised as soldiers hijacked Carole's vehicle at gunpoint. Prompted by God with the words "The Bibles," she showed one gunman, Jacob Ajok, his name in Genesis and told Bible stories for three and a half hours until both men confessed they had meant to kill her and received Jesus, leaving the car "as missionaries" with Bibles in hand; Favor later planted churches in Jacob's village on the Chad border. At the TOT gathering, 250 of Favor's 400 missionaries from seven nations trained for two months in a container-built campus. Salim, a terrorist Imam who had ordered the killing and imprisonment of Christians, arrived newly converted after four warning dreams; two missionaries he had persecuted publicly forgave him, and every hand went up when Carole asked who would go with him to the darkest Islamic nations. He now baptizes 20-30 Muslims a week in northern South Sudan (pp. 98-114).

**Chapters 12-13: Moses of Darfur.** Moses, a former Janjaweed rebel leader called "shepherd" by God in a dream, threw away his gun, started a Darfurian church in Juba even after Muslims abducted his wife and children (never found), and pastored a tarp-canopy slum congregation Carole met in 2015. Seven years later, in 2022, he returned ready to be sent; trained in the portable Bible School and Trauma Counseling, he evangelized Darfurian Muslim refugees in camps in South Sudan and then in eastern Chad near Abéché, baptizing hundreds in under a month and starting a church and missionary training school. Team member Richard was arrested by imams and miraculously rescued; convert Khadija was beaten by her brothers but declared she would never return to darkness (pp. 115-127).

**Chapters 14-15: brokenness, trauma healing, and the women.** Favor's trauma healing (Trauma Counseling) program, written in 2002 by an Australian Christian child psychologist and first implemented with Carole's Gulu household of about 16 in 2004, became the ministry's most effective evangelism gateway: classes of up to 2,000, a 98-99% salvation rate, entry into Muslim areas and among South Sudan's generals. Chapter 15 tells of Shama and the 50-woman co-op of Lopepe: two crops destroyed by cattle raiders, a revenge massacre of some 200 youths including Shama's son whose blood fouled the water hole, then forgiveness, a third planting, and the first harvest after three years. Carole took the Gospel to the raiders' camp, where eight chiefs received Jesus; the village received a well and a learning center for 200 children (pp. 128-141).

**Chapters 16-18: the radio tower, the spear master, and Chad.** Favour FM in Juba took five years of "giving birth to an elephant": four failed tower attempts (ambushed truckers, murdered crews, an absconding contractor, an unusable $10,000 mountaintop) before an Israeli firm shipped a 75-meter storm-proof quad booster days before war broke out in Israel; the station now broadcasts 24 hours a day into a city of 64 languages. Chapter 17 covers the outpost strategy: the Rumbek mission-station dedication before 600 people about ten years ago, the one-eyed witch doctor who ordered the team killed, and the recent confrontation with the feared spear master of Wulu County, whose ambushes (a swamp trap, armed motorcyclists at 9 p.m.) failed, ending with Carole praying peace over the gunman. Chapter 18 opens the Chad frontier: a missionary reports 91 unreached people groups in his area alone; Chad, with Lake Chad touching four countries where Boko Haram advances, becomes the gateway north, and Favor now has 200 trained Arabic-speaking missionaries from seven areas of Chad (pp. 141-163).

**Chapter 19: Kong and the first children.** Kong, a South Sudan military chaplain and Carole's self-appointed bodyguard ("two guns... the Bible... and a pistol and an AK47"), had led the Lost Boys of Sudan to freedom at fourteen. He died of hepatitis-B liver cirrhosis during the Covid era after a final mission of Nuer-Dinka trauma healing in which his class chose forgiveness during a deadly cattle raid and the attackers dropped their guns. Carole then recounts the first orphans of twenty years ago, now grown: Achen, Johnny, Charity (a veterinarian), Sylvia (a nurse), Stephen, George (a welder taught designs in dreams), Evan, Jacob and his twin, and Innocent, her first child soldier (2004), now a medic and worship leader in Germany. "The victims of war have become the instruments of revivals" (pp. 166-173).

**Chapters 20-23: calling, warfare, and the closing appeal.** Carole returns to her Oklahoma kitchen-sink calling and the three lessons of Peter walking on water: obedience over voices, taking thoughts captive, fixed focus on Jesus ("Stay fixed, focus on faith and don't fuss"). She recounts surviving cerebral malaria after being given 24 hours to live. Chapter 21 covers the girls fleeing prostitution and trafficking in Juba, up to 200 pouring into the House of Prayer, with testimonies from Precious, Akello, and Aber, plus the village-level antidote: Village Learning Centers and the three-year women's empowerment program (Ida's fifty-woman piggery co-op); government officials gave Favour a letter of credibility for succeeding "where the government has failed." Chapter 22 records her US pastor's warning, "Satan wants you dead," her resolve to finish strong like her father (still translating Scripture at ninety-two), and Jesus' picture of the Western church asleep "on the lap of Delilah," having lost the secret of its strength: prayer. Chapter 23 closes with Sadie, the 82-year-old whose $100,000 check built the first orphanage, and Carole's final appeal: "Send us all where no one else wants to go... Only with God's help! And yours" (pp. 175-203).

## Key places

- **Gulu, northern Uganda** — Carole's home base during and after the LRA war; House of Prayer; street kids center (250-300 teens); orphanage and school funded by Sadie's gift; first trauma healing program (2004); tiny radio station; national prayer gatherings in the main stadium.
- **Juba, South Sudan** — Capital; site of the July 2016 fighting; Favor's 20x20m compound with radio station (Favour FM), House of Prayer (warehouse property), offices; TOT container campus; street-children rescue (~20 feeding locations); girls' refuge; Juba prison Bible school; a city of 64 languages; no public power/water/sewage infrastructure at the time.
- **Kapoeta, Eastern Equatoria, South Sudan** — Toposa tribe outreach; the 800,000-person crusade region; the largest of the 10 states.
- **The "killer tribe" mountains** — Four hours from the crusade town; whole tribe converted.
- **Rumbek and Wulu County, South Sudan** — First House of Prayer/mission station dedication (600 people); one-eyed witch doctor; the spear master.
- **Majun area / Bomont region, Eastern Equatoria** — Territory of the hidden Kachipo tribe (2019 expedition), reaching to the Ethiopia border.
- **Murle territory, South Sudan (Pibor region)** — "The darkest, most demonic place on the map"; 2019 crusade; Japanese UN outpost.
- **Lopepe village, South Sudan** — 50-woman co-op; water-hole massacre; well, learning center for 200 children; one of almost 100 such villages.
