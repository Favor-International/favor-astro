# Dossier: Send Me Where No One Wants to Go (2017, by Barbara H. Martin - The Carole Ward Story)

Book by Barbara H. Martin, copyright 2017 by Favor International, Inc. Much of the text is drawn directly from Carole Ward's own newsletters to preserve her voice (Foreword, pp. 1, 4). Carole Ward is identified as Founder/Director of Favor of God Ministry (Uganda), Favour Africa Ministry (South Sudan), and Favor International, Inc. (USA) (p. 3). Partnership details at the end of the book: favorintl.org, mailing address in Bradenton, FL (p. 186).

## Chronological timeline (merged, deduplicated, with years and book page refs)

**Family background and early life**

- Early 1900s era: Carole's maternal grandparents serve as missionaries in China for 30 years; her mother is born there; the family is imprisoned when Communists invade Shanghai and later released as exchange prisoners of war (p. 8).
- 1960: Carole's parents go to the Philippine jungle as missionaries; they serve 48 years as missionaries and 60 years as Bible translators. Carole grows up in the Philippines as a missionary child (p. 8). Her mother later authors "Penetrating the Strongholds of Islam" (p. 154).
- Childhood/youth: Carole's grandmother, a New York intercessor, takes young Carole street-witnessing ("Let's go fishing, Honey") and passes on her intercessory calling ("Honey, I'm passing on the baton to you") (p. 154).
- Teen years onward: Carole returns to the US, marries at 17, earns a nursing degree, does therapeutic foster parenting for 20 years, raises 3 daughters, works as a nurse, opens a nursing center for Alzheimer's patients, and serves on her home church staff (p. 9).

**Call to Uganda (2002-2004)**

- 2002: Carole's first three-week mission trip to Kampala, Uganda, leaves her dissatisfied and plants the desire to serve there for life (p. 9).
- ~2002-2003 (second trip): She meets a seventy-year-old Campus Crusade missionary, founder of Lake Victoria Christian Center in Kampala, who later invites her by letter to run the two-year Bible school; she prays five months, then agrees to go for one year (pp. 9-10).
- End of 2003: Carole is named principal of Lake Victoria Christian Center (14-month intensive Bible training program, 80-acre campus, oversight of 42 portable Bible schools in five East African countries) (p. 10).
- 2003-2004 (during the Kampala year): Carole declares three days of fasting and prayer for the whole school with a 24-hour prayer chain: day one repentance, day two revival, day three restoration, transforming 150 students from Uganda, Kenya, Congo, Rwanda and Sudan (pp. 11-12).
- ~2004 (before going north): The US Embassy strongly warns her not to go to northern Uganda ("Joseph Kony's territory"), saying she would be crossed off the list and only return in a body bag; Christian organizations refuse to send her. Her pastor and church lay hands on her and send her; two widows agree to support her, and a man from a prayer group gives money for a car, her ticket, and a computer. On her bed she hears: "Ten said 'no'. Two said 'go'." Her oldest daughter slips Acts 26:19 into her hand at the airport (pp. 13-15).
- Early 2004: Carole moves to Gulu, a six-hour drive north of Kampala, just as the Uganda People's Defense Force launches a massive offensive against LRA bases in southern Sudan and the LRA retaliates against IDP camps. Five pastors welcome her; she is the only Westerner in the region (pp. 16-18).
- 2002 (context statistic): 90% of Gulu district's population had sought refuge in Internally Displaced People Camps housing 10,000-60,000 people each (p. 16). More than 2 million people lived in over 200 camps in Northern Uganda; the Ugandan Ministry of Health estimated 1,000 died weekly from malaria, starvation, ambushes and AIDS (p. 17). Over 35,000 children, mostly ages 8-15, were abducted by Kony's rebels (p. 17).

**Early Gulu ministry (2004-2005)**

- 2004+: Carole puts up a "House of Prayer" sign (7 am and 7 pm, 7 days a week), holds monthly retreats for pastors and wives, and trains team leaders who travel to over one hundred displaced-persons camps (each 10,000-75,000 people) across the North (pp. 18-19).
- Undated, Gulu years: The "night of fire": LRA rebels attack at night, burning huts near Carole's house; she prays and hears God say "Give them Bibles instead of bullets"; the next morning she comforts a widow whose girl died in the fire and whose husband and two boys were taken (pp. 6-16).
- Early ministry: Favor's camp work begins in Keyo camp with food, clothes and the Gospel; Carole meets a leper whose outstretched hands move her, and in a night vision Jesus says "If you dance with the lepers, I will heal them" (pp. 23-24).
- Early ministry: Driving alone through rebel ambush territory, God confronts her ("You are living in self-preservation instead of self-denial"), baptizes her in love, and permanently removes her fear (p. 25).
- 2004+ (night commuters): Carole witnesses ~15,000 children walking barefoot 5-8 km into Gulu each evening to sleep under army protection; God tells her, "Open a children's home for them" (pp. 41-42).
- ~2004-2005: Achang, an escaped LRA abductee (taken at 14; her brother, taken at 12, killed in an eight-hour battle), stumbles into the mission house during 7 am prayer, receives Jesus, and is healed over six months; she becomes the ministry's first Mama (pp. 43-44).
- Same era: Carole takes the first four orphaned sisters (ages 2, 6, 10, 12) into her own house, living on two widows' monthly support; soon a facility opens in Gulu and the home grows to 33 total orphans, with hundreds more helped outside the home (pp. 42-45).
- July 2005: Favor of God ministry founded (July 2007 is called its "second birthday," p. 78).
- 2005 (preceding Dec 15): Forty days of prayer chains mobilized in local churches and the House of Prayer; the government first refuses, then miraculously grants, permission to use the War Memorial Stadium (pp. 33-34).
- December 15, 2005: The Gulu National Prayer Gathering: over 1,000 people gather for three days of prayer in the Acholi War Memorial Stadium; government officials later credit this "stadium prayer" with paving the way to the end of the war (pp. 34-35, 37). Former Kony general Ochan later testifies Kony told him his power left him at that exact time (p. 89). The book dates ten years of peace in northern Uganda from this gathering (p. 141).
- December 2005: God tells Carole "the strong man of Gulu" (Awire, a demonized, violent street lunatic) must be bound; after a week of prayer and fasting, the team captures, delivers, bathes and clothes Awire; word of his healing breaks the demonic stronghold over Gulu (pp. 51-53). Awire stays with the ministry five or six years, then returns to his village totally healed and farms his family's land (p. 54).

**Expansion across the camps (2006)**

- January 2006: Month of prayer set aside in Gulu town (p. 35).
- January-February 2006: Seven houses of prayer established in seven camps, with a vision for prayer houses (24-hour prayer chains) in all 150 camps (pp. 35, 37).
- First part of 2006: Portable Bible schools begin in the camps, staffed by Bible school students and local pastors (p. 50).
- ~2006: Team of 12 sent to Ocet camp (the district where Kony grew up) for teaching, evangelism, prayer teams, Bible distribution and crusades (p. 36).
- ~2006: 110 students graduate from a two-month intensive Bible school in Opit camp (50,000+ people, second largest camp in the north); children's camp, medical outreach with free dental care, Jesus film shown in Acholi (p. 38).
- ~2006 (Anaka): Six months after Joseph Kony's followers curse camp Anaka (having been refused the most beautiful girl for sacrifice), strange fires fall from the sky burning single huts; after three weeks of prayer and fasting Carole leads 70 intercessors in a five-day campaign: the fires stop, hundreds are saved, 200+ babies are dedicated to Jesus, all five witch doctors convert, the school reopens, and 50-60 children are set free from demonic torment by 2 a.m. (pp. 55-61). Within a month Favor returns with a two-month portable Bible school, birthing a church (p. 61).
- A few months later: Patong camp calls about "fires falling"; Carole sends the new Anaka Christians instead of going herself; the fires stop (p. 61).
- ~2006: Staff grows (accounting, children's work, administration, teaching); a children's home compound with thirty-three beds is provided in Gulu; the Gulu prison chaplain asks Favor to add a women's prison ministry and requests as many Bibles as possible (pp. 62-63).
- 2006 year-end results: New Life Bible College first graduation (20 students, 14 town pastors); 4,343 Acholi Bibles distributed to sixty camps, Gulu town and 5 districts; seven two-month portable Bible schools in seven camps; 745 pastors/church leaders graduated; 4,108 souls saved door-to-door; two pastors' retreats and two Leadership Training Conferences; Houses of Prayer in ten IDP camps; two three-day prayer gatherings in Anaka; two Schools of Prayer in Gulu; monthly overnight stadium prayer; House of Hope Children's Home opened for permanent residence (33 children ages 1-18, 5 lady staff); Favor of God Primary School started by faith at government leaders' request; prison ministry two days a week (285 saved, 61 trained, 141 Acholi Bibles); trauma counseling began September 2006 (four camps, 576 people); Children's Mailbox Club (2,694 children saved, 2,369 graduated from 10-week classes) (pp. 68-70).
- ~2004-2009 (five years): Carole stays in her little office at the mission house training Acholi people as Kingdom leaders, sending them out daily and confronting cultural resistance to core values like integrity and honesty (p. 65).

**Growth years (2007-2009)**

- February 2007: A Bible College branch opens in Lira district (p. 68).
- 2007: Carole begins sending teams into Sudan with portable Bible schools and evangelistic outreaches after Sudanese pastors phone pleading for teaching and Bibles, one calling from atop a mango tree (p. 104).
- July 2007: Favor of God's second birthday; the ministry enters its third year (p. 78).
- ~2007 onward: The Empower trauma counseling program (developed by Dr. Robi of Australia; Favor was the first to implement it) helps 3,000 people in more than 12 camps; over the next five years over 40,000 go through it, 95% accepting Christ (pp. 70, 78).
- ~2007: Ojara, a university graduate from Kampala, humbles himself, accepts a $12/month wage, and becomes head of the twelve trauma counseling teams (pp. 79-80).
- 2007-2009: Described as the years of explosive ministry growth; "Still a General, but in God's Army" becomes the battle cry (p. 88).
- Late 2000s: Ochan, a Ugandan fighter pilot (Israel-trained, 20 years a government officer) captured by Kony and made one of his generals for 20 years, is recaptured in his sixties, saved at an open-door crusade in Gulu, and becomes a lead intercessor (pp. 88-90).
- Late 2000s: Village-by-village evangelism (notably Patong): witch doctors saved, hundreds of villagers converted, churches planted; on the drive home a staff leader's father (hospitalized after taking poison) is saved and healed, plus two other patients in the room (pp. 90-91).
- Late 2000s: The monthly ministry budget reaches about $50,000, run entirely on faith; an Oklahoma church calls out of the blue with a large gift for a ministry "which lives the book of Acts" (pp. 95-96).
- Undated (late 2000s): Ebola outbreak (with cholera, hepatitis, meningitis) goes undetected almost three months; WHO predicts up to 45,000 deaths in the camps within three weeks; nationwide united prayer is mobilized by radio, overnight stadium prayer in Gulu, and a week of prayer and fasting; on the Friday, Gulu hospital calls to say all five quarantined patients tested negative and the plague has stopped (pp. 96-98).
- Afterward: Gulu hospital sends dozens of terminally ill patients to the House of Prayer, where many are healed, including Odoch, a comatose end-stage HIV/occult-afflicted man; two years later Odoch has a communications/radio diploma and serves on the ministry team (pp. 98-100).
- Post-2006 (as war ends): IDP camps slowly disband after 20 years of war; the ministry becomes the community "help center," adding clothing distribution, medical and dental outreaches, and three-day Jesus Film crusades (p. 86).

**Trial and expansion into South Sudan (2010-2013)**

- c. 2008-2010: A team leader secretly works for two years to take over the ministry and dismiss Carole; a fatherly board member refuses her resignation ("You gave birth to this work"), the rebel and co-conspirators leave, and Carole forgives him. 2010 is a hard year of painful lessons (pp. 101-102).
- End of 2010: Carole personally joins the South Sudan mission teams after the Lord speaks to her clearly, following a vision of Jesus telling her to follow His blood-stained footprints (p. 104).
- July 2011: South Sudan wins independence from northern Sudan in a peaceful referendum after a sixty-two-year civil war (p. 103). Pastor Rico, a Darfur-born former Muslim, stops fighting with the SPLM and begins studying the Christian faith (p. 172).
- c. 2011: US and Kenyan teams help train trainers in Orality Bible Storytelling, equipping almost 100 indigenous believers from Congo, Sudan and northern Uganda (95% of rural Sudanese were non-literate) (p. 105).
- c. 2010-2011: Carole's first thirteen-hour trek to Torit: the car sinks into swamp mud three times (rescued by a tribe of small-statured men who receive Bibles); at the last checkpoint every soldier kneels and accepts Christ (pp. 106-108).
- November (c. 2011): Torit meetings produce reports of hundreds saved, blind eyes opened, crippled walking; "The book of Acts is repeating itself" (p. 108).
- c. 2011: Open-air crusade in Torit draws 30,000 the first night, 50,000 on following nights, and 500,000 counted by government officials the last night; witchdoctors burn their witchcraft tools (pp. 108-109).
- c. 2011: At the Governor of the State of Torit's request, the team enters feared mountain villages under military escort; an entire village of 500 souls receives salvation (p. 109).
- c. 2011: Crusades around Kapeota total over 800,000 attendees; Carole hosts a dinner (a whole cow) for 80 tribal chiefs, all of whom accept Jesus, birthing ongoing "government dinners" and her "root killers" teaching on destructive traditions (pp. 110-111).
- c. 2011: A woman in Juba, hearing of the Torit crusades, gives the ministry a brand-new Land Cruiser (pp. 111-112).
- c. 2011-2012 (Gulu): House of Hope boys move into the first finished building; the New Life Bible College building is completed; a grant funds Portable Bible schools and reactivates the Trauma Rehabilitation program; a dirt motorbike is donated; the clinic moves to a central rental site; a fish pond is dug on the ministry's ten acres; partnership begins with World Vision's Rural Christian Leadership Program; the House of Prayer for All Nations fills daily at noon and a building extension is funded (pp. 113-114).
- 2013: A big Islamic push into South Sudan from the inside; two tribal leaders pitted against each other cause division; China enters because of oil (p. 135).
- 2013: Carole organizes a pastors' and leaders' conference in Juba; over 300 attend, with a visiting US team doing teaching and medical outreaches; she delivers the "elephants or termites" teaching to discouraged Juba pastors (pp. 135-137).

**Radio, prayer gatherings, mega-crusades (2014-2015)**

- Before 2014: At a remote mountaintop government prison in Idi Amin's old castle at Patika, prisoners reveal they were saved through Carole's weekly radio broadcasts, birthing the vision for a Favor radio station (pp. 148-150).
- 2014: Favor FM 93.3, "The heartbeat of God," goes on the air in Gulu; dozens of listeners call in daily with testimonies. A woman named Aber is stopped from suicide by Pastor Francisco's on-air word of knowledge; she and her husband come to the station within an hour and are saved (pp. 151-152).
- July 2015: National Prayer Gathering in Juba: Carole drives alone (cannot afford $50 visas for the team); a 40-day fast is organized across 40 churches with Musa and Mudasia; the $20,000 culture center rental is supplied overnight by an American friend after one phone call; 77 hours of prayer (11 hours a day for 7 days: 2 days repentance, 2 days revival, 2 days restoration, 1 day praise); over 1,000 people gather, uniting all denominations and political leaders for the first time (pp. 138-140). Muslim opposition withdraws; storms are prayed away three times; a member of Parliament phones with news the Addis Ababa peace talks will be signed in August, after two years of war (pp. 142-144).
- Post-2014 (crusade era): Crusades in Rumbek, Wau and Torit under the banner "Jesus Works Miracles! Come, Dead or Alive": at Freedom Square, Rumbek, Deng (crippled 35 years, from birth) walks and a dead girl is raised; the Wau crusade draws 1.2 million people, the largest Favor has ever held; a wealthy Muslim woman from Khartoum is healed of a worn-out hip; Imams come to the Lord. All crusades are followed by portable Bible schools and church growth (pp. 155-158).
- After Rumbek: Carole confronts the most feared witch doctor in the Rumbek area during a blood ritual; the woman screams her demons have left and sends spearmen after the team, who escape by car (pp. 158-159).
- Same era: Susa, a Dinka refugee mother evicted from Sudan, walks seven days barefoot with two sons from Northern Bahr El Ghazal to reach a Favor crusade, arriving as the team packs up; leading her to Christ delays the team's departure and spares them from a deadly roadside ambush by armed cattle raiders (pp. 146-148).
- c. 2015-2016: A US ministry partner offers to build church buildings "as many as you want"; a donor commits to fund 1,000 church buildings in Northern Uganda and 1,000 in South Sudan; the first is built in Atiak on land dedicated by Loum, an LRA survivor; the village is transformed and later united in expelling a returning witchdoctor (pp. 160-162).

**America, return under fire, Juba House of Prayer (2016-2017)**

- Most of 2016: Carole travels through the US teaching and praying with Christians around the election; she describes God performing a miracle on Election Day 2016 (p. 165).
- Beginning of 2017: Carole returns to Uganda and immediately goes on to South Sudan despite authorities telling all foreigners to leave (p. 165).
- Early 2017: Violent food poisoning delays Carole and Martha's departure by a day, sparing them from a deadly rebel-vs-government battle at the border; they travel to Juba under military escort (one tank, three trucks, 30-40 armed soldiers) (p. 166).
- Early 2017: Carole and Martha find an empty downtown Juba warehouse ideal for a House of Prayer and radio station but cannot afford it (Juba property could cost at least $5 million USD) (pp. 167-168).
- February 2017: "Daddy Ray," an 80-year-old Australian minister, ministers at a large Juba conference of ~300 pastors, leaders, government officials and businessmen; over 200 Bibles go to Parliament officials, who weep: "We have to have God in government if this country is going to be healed" (p. 168).
- March 2017: The South Sudan government calls a National Prayer Conference to which Carole is invited (p. 169).
- 2017: Brokers offer the same Juba warehouse for purchase with a small down payment; it becomes Favor's first House of Prayer and radio station in South Sudan (p. 169).
- May 2017: Grand opening of the Juba House of Prayer with citywide festivities; audio and written Bibles given away (pp. 169-170).
- By 2017: Pastor Rico's Darfurian refugee fellowship in Juba numbers 70 believers, sending underground missionaries into Darfur in rotating teams of four for six-month terms; Carole commits Favor of God to stand with them (pp. 172-174).
- At time of writing (2017): Islam is on an aggressive march into South Sudan, mosques being built everywhere (some used as ammunition storage), Muslim schools paying parents one month's salary to send children; Favor teams still go north to the unreached and the Muslim world; Carole leads an eighty-member team (pp. 184-185).

## Carole's story arc (chapter by chapter)

**Chapter 1, The Night of Fire (pp. 6-19).** The book opens in Gulu, northern Uganda, with Carole awakened by an LRA rebel attack burning mud huts near her house. Weeping and praying through the night, she hears God say, "Give them Bibles instead of bullets," and receives the foundational instruction that His Word is light to be carried like candles across the North. The chapter then rewinds through her heritage: grandparents who spent 30 years as missionaries in China and were imprisoned in Shanghai, parents who served 48 years in the Philippine jungle, and her own American years as a nurse, therapeutic foster parent of 20 years, and mother of three daughters. A 2002 mission trip to Kampala ignites her call; a veteran Campus Crusade missionary recruits her to run Lake Victoria Christian Center, where as principal she leads a three-day fast that sparks revival among 150 students from five nations. There she learns her core philosophy: if she goes to one village, God will be there, but if she trains a hundred to go, soon He will be everywhere.

**Chapter 1, continued: going north.** Feeling called to Joseph Kony's war zone, Carole is refused by every mission organization and warned by the US Embassy that she would return only "in a body bag." Praying on her bed she hears, "Ten said 'no'. Two said 'go'. I will give you a promised land." Her pastor sends her with laid-on hands; two widows and a single donor fund her. In early 2004 she drives six hours north to Gulu, arriving amid a military offensive and LRA retaliation, the only Westerner in a region where 90% of the district population lived in displacement camps and a thousand people died weekly. She opens a House of Prayer (7 am and 7 pm, seven days a week) in her small brick house and begins training local pastors and leaders who carry the gospel into over a hundred camps. To a pastor who asks if she will just take pictures and leave, she answers, "I didn't come here to sow a seed, I came to sow my life."

**Chapter 2, Dancing with Lepers (pp. 21-30).** A dream of washing the muddy feet of a bride confirms her call to help dress northern Uganda as Christ's bride. Ministry begins in Keyo camp with food, clothes and the Gospel amid starvation, disease and 120 witches and warlocks in the Gulu area. Meeting a leper who reaches out to her leads to a night vision in which Jesus says, "If you dance with the lepers, I will heal them." Driving alone through ambush territory, God confronts her fear ("You are living in self-preservation instead of self-denial"); she asks to be baptized in His love, waves of liquid peace wash over her, and she testifies she was never afraid again. The chapter carries the full testimony of Opio, abducted at 15, beaten 150 times, forced to carry a severed head 35 miles, who escaped in 2003, was rehabilitated at World Vision's center in Gulu, attended a Portable Bible School in 2004, and became Carole's "first son" and a ministry leader.

**Chapter 3, Fire Meets Sacrifice (pp. 31-38).** The ministry shifts decisively to spiritual warfare through prayer: the mission house fills with people praying 7 am to 7 pm, and forty days of prayer chains precede the landmark Gulu National Prayer Gathering of December 15, 2005, when over 1,000 people pray for three days in the Acholi War Memorial Stadium. Government officials later credit this stadium prayer with paving the way to the end of the war. Seven houses of prayer open in seven camps that January and February; a team of 12 evangelizes Ocet camp, Kony's home district; and in Opit camp (50,000+ people) 110 students graduate from a two-month intensive Bible school alongside children's camps, medical outreach and the Jesus film in Acholi.

**Chapter 4, Treasures of Darkness (pp. 40-47).** Roughly fifteen thousand "night commuter" children walk into Gulu each evening to sleep under army protection. God tells Carole to open a children's home, beginning with "total orphans." With no funding she takes four orphaned sisters (ages 2, 6, 10 and 12) into her own house, living on two widows' monthly gifts, eating many days only potatoes and water. Achang, an escaped LRA abductee healed over six months at the mission house, becomes the first Mama. The home grows to thirty-three total orphans, a Gulu facility opens, and later 14 acres are purchased for the House of Hope children's village. The children become prayer warriors: after a year of daily prayer and Wednesday fasting by her thirty-two brothers and sisters, three-year-old Akella, whose bones a Kampala surgeon called "mush," walks normally.

**Chapter 5, on the Word and the strong man (pp. 49-54).** God's directive "Go north and just turn the light on, for light dispels darkness" drives Bible distribution by the tens of thousands (eventually 60,000+ in 12+ languages), a Bible College training pastors, and portable Bible schools in the camps from early 2006. In December 2005 God identifies Awire, the demonized "strong man" of Gulu; after a week of prayer and fasting the team captures, delivers, bathes and clothes him. His visible healing breaks the demonic stronghold over the city, unleashing a floodgate of new ministries, Bible studies and churches; hundreds bring their mentally ill to the House of Prayer. Awire studies the Bible with the ministry five or six years and returns home totally healed.

**Chapter 6, Anaka (pp. 55-61).** After Kony's followers curse camp Anaka for refusing to hand over a girl for sacrifice, unexplained fires fall from the sky, each burning a single hut, and the school closes over children's unexplained seizures. The chief phones: "Can your God help us?" After three weeks of prayer and fasting, Carole leads seventy intercessors in a five-day campaign: when flames fall again during a monsoon, the prayer warriors take authority and no flame ever falls again; hundreds are saved, over two hundred babies dedicated to Jesus, all five witch doctors converted, the school reopens, and 50-60 children are delivered from demons by 2 a.m. When Patong camp later suffers the same phenomenon, Carole sends the new Anaka Christians instead of going herself, and the fires stop.

**Chapter 7, A Bride out of Ashes (pp. 62-77).** Prayer and worship become the foundation of everything: two hours every morning, noon prayer downtown, all-night Friday prayer, Wednesday fasting, and quarterly weeks of prayer and fasting. Carole spends five years in her little office training Acholi people as Kingdom leaders, insisting on "Kingdom culture" values of integrity and honesty against cultural resistance. The 2006 year-end summary records the Bible college's first graduation, 4,343 Bibles distributed, 745 bush-school graduates, 4,108 saved door-to-door, ten camp Houses of Prayer, the House of Hope home, the Favor of God Primary School started by faith, prison ministry (285 saved), the September 2006 start of trauma counseling, and thousands of children reached through the Mailbox Club. Latim's testimony follows: abducted at eight, twelve years in the LRA, sole survivor of battles and ambushes, buried three days by a bomb blast, now calling Carole "Mum" and leading with Favor. Carole also writes candidly of crushing loneliness, exhaustion and being preyed on as a woman alone in a man's world, comforted by her poem "I Know You're There."

**Chapter 8, Taking the Kingdom by Force (pp. 78-87).** At Favor's second birthday in July 2007, after twenty-one years of civil war had pushed 85% of the northern population into camps, the Empower trauma counseling program (developed by Dr. Robi of Australia, first implemented by Favor) reaches 3,000 people in more than 12 camps and, over five years, over 40,000, with 95% accepting Christ. Testimonies include abducted women, a mother whose four children were clubbed to death, and a seventeen-year-old former abductee, all finding forgiveness. Ojara, an arrogant university graduate, humbles himself, accepts $12 a month, and heads the twelve trauma counseling teams. The Mission House becomes the hub: staff of over twenty, library, Bible distribution center, prison ministry in men's and women's prisons, Bible studies in schools, the university and military barracks (soldiers carry Bibles to Somalia, Sudan, Rwanda and Congo), bi-monthly pastor retreats, medical and dental outreaches, and three-day Jesus Film crusades.

**Chapter 9, Still a General, but in God's Army (pp. 88-94).** In the explosive growth years of 2007-2009, Ochan, a Ugandan fighter pilot who served twenty years as a government officer and then twenty years as one of Kony's generals, is saved at a Gulu crusade in his sixties and becomes a lead intercessor. He testifies that Kony fasted and pleaded with Satan to keep his powers, and that Kony told him the power had left him at the exact time of Carole's first stadium prayer. The team evangelizes village by village, including Patong, where witch doctors are saved and churches planted; on the drive home a staff leader's dying father is saved and healed along with two other patients in the room.

**Chapter 10, Midnight Wrestlers (pp. 95-100).** The ministry runs a roughly $50,000 monthly budget entirely on faith; when funds run out, God tells Carole to "keep bringing in the vessels... there will always be enough oil," and an Oklahoma church phones with a large gift for a ministry "which lives the book of Acts." When Ebola breaks out alongside cholera, hepatitis and meningitis, and the WHO predicts up to 45,000 deaths in the camps within three weeks, Carole calls the nation to prayer by radio; after a week of prayer and fasting, Gulu hospital reports all five quarantined patients tested negative and the plague has stopped. The hospital then sends dozens of terminally ill patients to the House of Prayer, where many are healed, including the comatose HIV patient Odoch, who two years later serves on the ministry team, saying, "God gave me my life back and now I want to give it away."

**Chapter 11, Follow in His Footprints (pp. 101-114).** After a team leader's two-year plot to seize the ministry is exposed, a fatherly board member refuses Carole's resignation: "You gave birth to this work." 2010 becomes a hard year of lessons, and forgiveness. A vision of Jesus telling her to follow His blood-stained footprints, leaving the 99 for the one, formalizes the South Sudan outreach that had begun with teams in 2007. Orality Bible Storytelling equips nearly 100 indigenous believers; former SPLM soldier Ohide becomes a key pastor. Carole's first thirteen-hour trek to Torit ends with checkpoint soldiers kneeling to accept Christ; Torit crusades swell from 30,000 to 500,000, Kapeota-area crusades total over 800,000, a dinner sees 80 tribal chiefs accept Jesus, and a Juba woman donates a new Land Cruiser. Back in Gulu, House of Hope's first building opens, the Bible College is completed, and partnerships and facilities multiply.

**Chapter 14, Elephants or Termites (pp. 130-137).** The book turns fully to South Sudan: its Cushite biblical heritage (mentioned over 140 times in Scripture; Christianity governed Cush from 543-1315 AD), sixty-plus years of civil war, the 2011 independence, and the 2013 Islamic push and tribal division. Chapter 16 traces the radio ministry: prisoners in Idi Amin's old castle at Patika reveal they were saved through her weekly broadcasts, birthing Favor FM 93.3, launched in Gulu in 2014 and called "The heartbeat of God"; a word of knowledge on air stops Aber's suicide. **Chapter 17, Jesus Works Miracles! Come, Dead or Alive (pp. 155-160).** Crusades in Rumbek, Wau and Torit run under posters reading "JESUS WORKS MIRACLES! COME, DEAD OR ALIVE." At Rumbek's Freedom Square, Deng, crippled from birth for 35 years, walks and returns the next day with about 400 villagers; a dead girl is raised. The Wau crusade draws 1.2 million people, the largest ever, with a wealthy Muslim woman from Khartoum healed of a ruined hip ("Allah couldn't do this, but Jesus did!") and Imams converted. Carole confronts the most feared witch doctor near Rumbek mid-ritual; the woman's demons leave and her spearmen chase the team off. A US partner's offer to build churches, "as many as you want," launches the church-building initiative.

**Chapters 18-19, building and the missionary army (pp. 161-180).** A donor commits to a thousand church buildings in Northern Uganda and a thousand in South Sudan; the first rises in Atiak on land dedicated by LRA survivor Loum, transforming the village. After most of 2016 in the US, Carole returns in early 2017 and enters South Sudan against official warnings; food poisoning providentially delays her past a border battle, and she reaches Juba under military escort. God provides a downtown Juba warehouse that becomes Favor's first House of Prayer and radio station in South Sudan, opened May 2017; the February 2017 Daddy Ray conference draws ~300 leaders and puts 200+ Bibles in the hands of Parliament officials. Pastor Rico, a Darfur refugee who survived imprisonments and a death sentence, leads 70 believers sending underground missionaries into Darfur; Carole commits Favor to stand with them. Chapter 19 lays out the model: an indigenous African missionary army raised through portable Bible schools (over 7,000 trained, over 60,000 Bibles distributed), at a fortieth of the cost of sending western missionaries, all born in a rented Gulu prayer room, with prayer as the engine of everything.

**Chapter 20, His Light in the Darkness (pp. 181-186).** Author Barbara H. Martin closes with a direct word to readers: God put one "little, insignificant candle" into a dark room, one lone white woman filled with His light, to help raise the Ugandan and South Sudanese people out of the ashes of war and satanic oppression. Barbara contrasts Western spiritual starvation (not believing what God says) with pre-gospel Uganda's (not knowing what God says), warns of Islam's aggressive march into South Sudan, and calls readers to fervent prayer and financial partnership with Carole's eighty-member team.
